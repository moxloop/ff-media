hook|You've got a spare hundred pounds a month. Should it go on the mortgage, or into investments? Over twenty-five years, if investing returns six per cent after fees, it finishes about eight thousand, seven hundred pounds ahead. At four per cent, overpaying wins by about seven thousand. It's a see-saw, and one number decides which way it tips. You already know it.
roadmap|Last time, we saw what overpaying one hundred pounds a month does to our example mortgage. Today, the bigger question: is that the best home for the money? First, what overpaying really earns you. Then what investing can earn, and what fees and tax take off the top. Then we'll race the two over twenty-five years. This is a simple example, not advice, and there's no single right answer.
guaranteed|Here's the mortgage again. Two hundred thousand pounds, over twenty-five years, at five per cent: one thousand, one hundred and sixty-nine pounds, eighteen pence a month. Every pound you still owe costs you five per cent a year. So every pound you overpay stops costing you that. Pay off an extra hundred pounds, and that's about five pounds of interest you never pay, every year. Think of overpaying as a savings account paying a guaranteed five per cent, from a bank that can't say no. No fees. No tax. No bad years.
expected|Investing is a different animal. Over long stretches, a broad spread of shares has tended to grow faster than that. But it's an expected return, not a promised one. Some years it jumps. Some years it drops, sometimes sharply. Picture a balance scale. On one side, a solid block: a guaranteed five per cent. On the other, a block that won't sit still. It might be heavier. It might be lighter. You only find out at the end.
fees|And the headline return isn't what you keep. Fees come out every year, like a dripping tap. Say your investments grow at seven per cent, and charges take one. You keep six. On a hundred pounds a month over twenty-five years, that turns about eighty-one thousand pounds into about sixty-nine thousand, three hundred. Nearly twelve thousand pounds, gone to fees. Tax can take another slice, unless the money sits in tax-free or tax-relieved accounts where you live. So the number to compare with your mortgage rate is the return after fees and tax.
setup|So let's set up a fair race. Two people, the same mortgage, the same spare hundred pounds. Think of two jars. Person A overpays. Their jar stays empty for years, but the mortgage is gone at month two hundred and fifty-eight, three and a half years early. From then on, the whole one thousand, two hundred and sixty-nine pounds, eighteen pence a month goes into the jar. Person B pays the mortgage as normal, and drops a hundred pounds into the jar for all three hundred months. Same money out, every month. Both end with the mortgage gone. The only question: whose jar is fuller?
